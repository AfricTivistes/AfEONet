**1. Money Is Distorting Political Competition**

The growing commercialisation of politics, vote-buying, opaque party financing, high nomination fees, illicit financial flows and the abuse of state resources are increasingly distorting political competition and excluding those without financial power.

**The solution:** strengthen political finance transparency through disclosure of contributions and expenditure, enforceable spending limits, oversight of digital campaign financing, monitoring of state resources and stronger enforcement of anti-corruption standards. The African Model Law on Political Finance Transparency provides an important foundation for this work.

**2. Civic Space for Election Observation Is Shrinking**

Citizen observers face delayed accreditation, restrictive regulations, judicial harassment, intimidation and other forms of retaliation in several countries. Yet independent observation remains essential to transparency, accountability and public trust.

**The solution:** protect citizen observation as a legitimate democratic activity and ensure that observers can operate throughout the electoral cycle—from voter registration and campaigns to political finance, voting, results and post-election follow-up. Accreditation should be transparent, timely, affordable and non-discriminatory.

**3. Technology Is a Double-Edged Sword**

Digital technologies and AI can improve electoral administration and citizen access to information, but deepfakes, disinformation, algorithmic manipulation, cyber threats and surveillance can also undermine public trust and distort political choices.

**The solution:** promote transparent technology procurement, independent technical audits, algorithmic accountability and responsible platform practices, while developing African approaches to the responsible use of AI in elections.

**4. The Implementation Gap Persists**

Africa has strong democratic and human-rights standards, but weak enforcement, limited institutional capacity and political interference continue to create a gap between commitments and practice. Election recommendations too often recur from one electoral cycle to another without meaningful action.

**The solution:** establish clear follow-up mechanisms with timelines, measurable indicators and public reporting so that election recommendations become actionable reforms rather than recurring observations.

**5. Building Stronger African Solidarity**

No single institution can protect electoral integrity alone. The Summit called for stronger collaboration among the African Union, Regional Economic Communities, electoral management bodies, civil society and observer networks. The launch of ROCiDAC, connecting Central African civil society and election-observation organisations to the wider AfEONet network, demonstrated the value of regional solidarity.

**From Diagnosis to Action**

The Accra Summit ultimately called for a shift from diagnosis to implementation, from commitments to measurable action, and from fragmented interventions to stronger African-led solidarity and solutions.

Electoral integrity is ultimately about protecting the agency, dignity and voice of African citizens. Achieving this requires transparent political finance, protected civic space, accountable technology, professional and independent citizen observation, responsive institutions and sustained follow-up after elections.

**The message from Accra is clear:** protecting electoral integrity means protecting the democratic conditions that allow citizens to freely and meaningfully exercise their political choices.
